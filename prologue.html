What happens

A fourteen-year-old boy called Yuhanna plays the ney at an excavation outside Mosul. An Englishman, Edmund Manners, hears him, writes the tune down, and hires him. One afternoon a workman’s foot goes through the floor of a trench and cold air comes up out of the dark. That night four people go down into a room nobody has mapped. The walls are covered in writing. There’s a stone in the middle with a stain on it, and a black door with seven holes in it.

Four go down. Three come up.

The real history

The hill that isn’t a hill

Kuyunjik is a real mound across the Tigris from Mosul. Underneath it is Nineveh, capital of the Assyrian Empire. Ancient cities in the Middle East were mostly mud brick, and when they fell down the rubble piled up for centuries until the whole city looked like a hill. Archaeologists call these mounds tells.

Rassam and Smith

Both are real. Hormuzd Rassam was an Assyrian Christian from Mosul who dug at Nineveh for the British Museum and found part of King Ashurbanipal’s library of clay tablets. George Smith taught himself cuneiform in his lunch breaks, found the Babylonian flood story on a tablet in 1872, and died of fever near Aleppo in 1876. Edmund Manners is made up.

The ney and bayati

The ney is a reed flute played across the top edge rather than into it. Six holes on the front, one at the back. Bayati is a maqam, a mode used in Arabic, Turkish and Persian music. Its second note sits between two piano keys, a quarter tone, and that narrow step is what gives it its sound.

The king who is not the king

The three carvings on the wall are based on a real Assyrian ritual, the šar pūhi, or substitute king. When an eclipse was read as a death sentence on the king, someone else was put on the throne to take the sentence, and the real king hid, called “the farmer”. Letters to King Esarhaddon describe it. One substitute, Damqi, is recorded as having “gone to his fate”.

“It means the king who is not the king,” he said. “The one they put on the throne to die.”

Music written in clay

Mesopotamian lyres had nine strings, and clay tablets survive that name the strings and explain how to tune them. The Hurrian Hymn from Ugarit, around 1400 BC, is the oldest substantially complete piece of written music we have.
